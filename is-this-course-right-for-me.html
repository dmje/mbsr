Is this course right for me?

An eight-week mindfulness course asks for a real commitment: weekly sessions, and formal practice at home on most days. Before you begin, it is worth being honest with yourself about whether this is the right time, and whether this kind of course is the right support for you.

What the course asks of you

  • Attendance at the weekly sessions, as many of the eight as you can manage.
  • Formal practice of around 30 to 45 minutes, most days, using the guided recordings.
  • Informal practice: small moments of awareness woven into ordinary daily activities.
  • A willingness to look at your own experience with curiosity, including the bits you would rather not see.

Most people find the hardest part is not the meditation itself but the time. It is genuinely difficult to set aside this much practice time, and it is normal to struggle with it. The course materials include suggestions for making practice realistic, and it is better to practice a little every day than a lot twice a week.

When to take extra care

Mindfulness is a powerful thing to turn towards your own mind, and for most people the benefits are real and well supported by research. But paying close attention to your experience can also bring difficult things to the surface. You should take particular care, and talk to the teacher before starting, if any of the following apply to you:

  • You are currently experiencing severe depression, anxiety, or another acute mental health difficulty.
  • You have experienced psychosis, or you are receiving treatment for a psychiatric condition.
  • You have recently gone through a bereavement, trauma, or another major life upheaval.
  • You are in early recovery from addiction.
  • You have a history of significant trauma. Focused attention practices such as the body scan can occasionally stir up traumatic memories, and a course like this is sometimes better approached when things are more settled.
  • You are experiencing suicidal thoughts, in which case this course is not the right support right now. Contact your GP, or a service such as Samaritans (116 123, free at any time in the UK), today.

None of these mean you can never do the course. They mean it is worth a conversation first, with the teacher and with your GP or therapist, so the course is timed and supported well for you.

What this course is not

It is not therapy. It does not diagnose or treat mental health conditions, and it is not a substitute for professional care. It is an educational course: you are learning a skill, and the practice you do between sessions is where the learning happens.

Where to get help

If you are already under the care of a GP, community mental health team, or therapist, keep them in the picture. If during the course you find practice is consistently making things worse rather than better, stop and speak to the teacher. Difficult experiences are common, and most pass quickly, but you should not have to weather them alone or in silence.
