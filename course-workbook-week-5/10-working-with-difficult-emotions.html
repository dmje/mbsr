Working with difficult emotions

So far the course has mostly asked you to notice your experience and let it move through you. This week we take a further step: turning towards what is difficult, deliberately and gently, using the body as the anchor.

Why turn towards it?

The instinct, when something hurts emotionally, is to push it away: distract, brood, argue with it, numb it out. In the short term this is human and understandable. Over time, though, avoidance keeps the difficult thing in charge. It waits behind the avoidance, getting heavier.

Turning towards does not mean diving in, or wallowing, or forcing yourself to feel more of something you can barely bear. It means dropping the struggle with it, and letting it be here as one more thing to be aware of. Two things tend to happen when you do this: the emotion becomes workable, and you discover you can feel it without it sweeping you away.

The practice

  1. Find it in the body. An emotion is not just a story in the head: anger lives somewhere in your chest, throat, jaw or stomach; anxiety often buzzes in the belly or the hands. Find where this emotion has a physical address.

  2. Soften around it. Rather than bracing against the sensation, soften the muscles in and around that area. You are not trying to make the sensation change or leave. You are just stopping clenching around it.

  3. Soothe yourself. Bring some warmth to the experience. This can be as simple as a hand placed on the place where it hurts, or a phrase said quietly to yourself: "This is hard. It is here. May I be kind to myself in this."

  4. Allow it to be as it is. Let the sensation be as intense or as quiet as it actually is. Watch whether it changes over a few breaths. Usually it does: sensations in the body live and move, tighten and release, even when the story about them stays stuck on repeat.

Not a technique for overwhelm

There is a difference between a difficult feeling you can sit beside, and a feeling that floods you. If turning towards tips you into overwhelm, that is information, not failure: come back to the breath, ground yourself in ordinary sensation, and practise with something smaller. Over time, with regular practice, what felt like flooding often becomes something you can approach gradually.

Try this once this week with a mild irritation, not your greatest sorrow. Start where the stakes are low. The skill is the same at any size.
