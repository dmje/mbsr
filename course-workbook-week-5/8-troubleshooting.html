Troubleshooting

You may find that you need to experiment quite a lot with how best to sit - don’t worry too much about adapting your position. However, fidgeting can become a bit of an obsession - it’s a bit like noticing an itch: you itch it, and then find another one and then another, and before you know it your whole body is covered in itches! If you feel you really need to move because of pain, pins and needles or an itch, that’s fine - but as you get more into the practice you will begin to see the benefit of sitting still, so try not to fidget.

If you do have to move, try and do it mindfully. Notice the sensation that is suggesting you need to move: “I have an itch. I need to itch it”. However before you move, try to bring mindful attention to the sensation itself, as well as the urge to be rid of it. Try and give it some space, feel around it, into it. What does an “itch” actually feel like? See if you can sense a “feeling tone”: does it feel pleasant, unpleasant or neutral? Do you feel aversion towards the sensation? Does the urge to be rid of it feel like something in the body? Can you try to be ok with these unpleasant or irritating sensations, simply observing them until they pass away in their own time?

Restlessness can become a serious impediment to our practice if we are not aware of it. A distracted mind can quickly become restless, and a restless mind is full of stories justifying why sitting for even a second longer is simply intolerable. Perhaps we become obsessed with our to-do list, remembering all the important tasks we haven’t yet done, or we fixate on some discomfort in the body, catastrophising about its possible severity. Either way, restlessness is an extremely deeply ingrained habit of the mind that exacerbates our suffering and tests our resolve to sit.

It is often hard to notice the underlying feeling of restlessness as the root of this torrent of stories. So if you find yourself struggling to sit for any longer and you are about to give up, try to ask ‘is there restlessness in the mind and body?’, and if so investigate what this feels like. The enemy of restlessness is beginner’s mind, or curiosity. Have you ever truly noticed what it is really like to feel restless? You may have felt it a million times already but have you ever even once become conscious of all of its intricacies and details in this way?

Bringing a sense of curiosity and mystery to whatever is arising in the mind and body is an excellent way to strengthen mindfulness and return to the practice with renewed vigour. A curious mind is an open mind, and an open mind is a mind without judgement, better able to accept things as they are. If we let go of struggle and aversion when restlessness arises, learning to accept restlessness as it is, then we quickly find that like all things it passes on its own.

Common feelings and some pointers

Feeling Response
I have an itch and need to scratch it Before you immediately rush to scratch, feel into the itch. What does it feel like? Where exactly in the body is it? Is it hot, or cold, or tingling? If you sit with the sensation for a bit, what happens? Does it get bigger, or smaller, or stay the same? When you finally reach to scratch - do it mindfully, slowly, with attention.
My foot / leg / arm is going to sleep Notice the sensation. Is it entirely unpleasant? What does “unpleasant” mean? Are you thinking about the sensation now, or what it could be in the future? Do you feel aversion or resistance towards it that is making it worse?
I keep thinking about work When you notice that your mind has wandered, feel into this. Do you feel cross with yourself? Have you labelled yourself a “bad meditator”?
My kids are making a noise and it’s distracting me Can you notice what it is about the noise that is distracting you? If you focus your mind on the sense of distraction, is it the noise itself or the story you are telling yourself that is making you cross with your children: “they always do this”, “I’ve told them before”, etc? If you focus on the noise, can you see it simply as it appears - as transitory, constantly changing vibrations in the air?
I’ve got so much to do! Why am I sitting here struggling at doing nothing? Look into your sense of frustration. What does frustration really feel like? Try to see yourself and these thoughts compassionately, as just another manifestation of your stress and suffering. Realise fully that this time is set aside for your own well-being, it is not a negation of other time.

When you have your sitting position and have settled into it, begin by noticing the breath. You may feel this in your chest, or in your stomach, or around your nose. Keep your mouth closed, and breathe through your nose - unless of course you have a cold in which case it’s fine to breathe through your mouth!

Use your breath to anchor yourself. Don’t change it - don’t breathe more deeply, but just notice your breath coming in and going out. Almost feel it arrive like the tide, all the way in, and then to nothing as it turns and all the way out again.

One thing you may want to do is to notice where you are breathing to. Most Westerners are fairly obsessed with body shape and as a consequence, we tend to breathe into our chests and not into our bellies. But take a moment to watch a baby or a dog deep in sleep and you’ll see that their stomach rises when they breathe in, and falls as they breathe out. They have none of the pretensions that we have - they just breathe!

Just One Breath

Sharon Salzburg, a well known meditation teacher in the US, has a couple of useful tips for breathing meditators. Firstly, she uses the phrase just one breath as a sort of mantra and guide. Don’t be concerned with future breaths, or past ones. We are not trying to ‘get anywhere’ or reach some special state with this practice, just notice the breath as it is. Don’t worry about the fact your mind just wandered and you only just now found your focus again - just be with this breath, right now, that is all there is, just one breath.

The second useful tip she talks about a lot is acceptance. Our minds will wander. Even the most seasoned meditators who have sat for thousands or even tens of thousands of hours will find their minds wandering off into other things and away from their focus. It’s inevitable with the busyness that we all have to deal with.

Salzburg says this - just accept and begin again - and she makes a big thing of this moment of noticing being the point of the practice.

So whereas we all (not just beginning meditators!) have the tendency to beat ourselves up when our minds wander: “I’m no good at this! I’ve just spent 20 minutes thinking about work!”, Salzburg teaches us that this moment when we notice the wandering is the moment of our practice. It’s when we get the chance to accept and bring our minds back into focus.

Watching the Mind

Meditation is the process of strengthening this ‘mental-muscle’ of returning to the present moment, and then resting in that moment. It is a repetitive process, just like strengthening any muscle is a process of repeated exercise. Our mind wanders, we recognise that it has wandered and we bring it back to the present - this just is meditation. So in a sense our minds have to wander in order for us to practice bringing them back.

Don’t rush this process of returning to the breath, it can be wise to linger for a moment to give the thinking mind some space to settle, perhaps noticing what stories your mind has been lost in, e.g. planning, judging, remembering, before gently returning to the breath with kindness for yourself.

When thoughts come to mind that are uncomfortable, sad or painful, don’t try to turn them away. Don’t lock your mind against thoughts, or turn your thinking to something else. Instead, just notice the thought. Notice how it makes you feel, how these feelings show up in the body, and try to welcome this, allowing this all to be there, without judgement and without adding anymore second arrows.

Be gentle with yourself! Like all of us, you have been your thoughts for many, many years, completely wrapped up in the stories you tell yourself. You are used to getting carried away, being lost in thought - this practice is simple but not easy. You will find yourself 5 or 10 or 20 minutes later having returned from a long journey into your work or home life, much as you find yourself having driven for 50 miles on the motorway with no idea what you’ve been doing all that time. But this is ok. Just return to the breath without judgement.

It is no use trying to whip your mind into line, jerking your attention back to the breath whenever it wanders. This harsh treatment is likely only going to increase any frustration when you find your mind wandering, and can lead you to become disheartened with your practice.

Cultivating this light, gentle touch is going to be a great friend to you, both in life and during your practice. Practice is a space for experimentation and a chance to be playful in the way we relate to our minds and respond to difficulties. So be kind, and don’t forget to laugh at yourself!

Remember, at the most general level wanting things to be other than how they in fact are is probably why you are struggling. Mindfulness meditation is a practice of welcoming whatever arises as best you can. So when difficulties arise try to ask yourself as earnestly as you can ‘is there anything in my experience that I am finding it difficult to accept?’

When thoughts do enter our minds, we can practice watching the thinker - we see the thoughts as they are, as transient, random, illusory.
