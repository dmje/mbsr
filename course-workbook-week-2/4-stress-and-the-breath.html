Stress and the Breath

Our breathing is such an important aspect of our lives and our health. However, when we suffer with stress or anxiety, our breathing can become impaired or restricted. In particular, stress can cause rapid and shallow breathing, which is commonly known as hyperventilation. This type of breathing is moved predominantly by the upper chest, back and shoulder muscles; muscles that were not designed for the purpose of breathing.

Hyperventilation causes you to exhale more than you inhale which leads to a loss of carbon dioxide. This can result in a narrowing of the blood vessels that supply blood to the brain, causing headaches, dizziness, and confusion - not ideal if you are already feeling stressed and anxious! By losing carbon dioxide, the blood also becomes more alkaline which affects the movement and flow of important elements in our blood which can lead to illness and disease.

Developing a habit of this rapid and shallow breathing from the chest (hyperventilation) can have a range of long term physical and mental effects that can lead to illness and disease. Illness and disease then lead to greater stress, which will cause further hyperventilation which reinforces illness and disease and so on in a vicious cycle.

Abdominal Breathing

As we saw, when we are stressed our breathing becomes very shallow and rapid, known as ‘hyperventilation’. This breathing pattern comes mostly from the upper chest taking over our breathing. 

Hyperventilating is surprisingly common, with figures suggesting 40% of people are hyperventilating when attending the GP.

However, this is not our natural way of breathing. It is the muscles of the diaphragm, a dome-shaped muscle located below the ribcage and just above the belly, that naturally aids the body breathing. If you have ever watched a baby sleeping in their cot, you’ll see the gentle rise and fall of their abdomen or belly as the breath goes in and out.

When the diaphragm contracts it flattens down, making space in the chest and creating a vacuum that sucks air into the lungs. And when the diaphragm relaxes it returns to its original size and shape forcing air out of the lungs.

Breathing from the abdomen, sometimes known as ‘diaphragmatic breathing’, actually releases hormones responsible for calming and relaxing the body and mind. It activates our parasympathetic nervous system sometimes known as the 'rest and digest' function.

Our breath is such a powerful ally, having a string of physiological benefits that naturally combat an over-activate nervous system, and break the stress-cycle.

Somewhere along the way many of us have lost the habit of abdominal breathing, with its natural soothing effects on the body and mind. Our longer meditation practices are a great way to return to our natural, calming state of breathing. However, we can also use a more specific abdominal breathing practice when we find ourselves getting stressed out during the day.

Because of the physiological benefits of abdominal breathing, if you find yourself stressed and anxious and need to take a moment, doing a short abdominal breathing practice can help calm the nervous system and ground you back in the body, breaking out of the stress response.

First notice how the breath is right now. Is the breath fast and shallow? Does it feel smooth or uneven? Where do you feel the breath in the body right now? Do you feel it in the belly or the chest?

Now gently place a hand on your belly and without trying to change the breath, take a moment to feel into the breath at this part of the body. Let the body breathe itself. Do you feel movement at the belly? Is there any rising and falling? Settle the mind in these sensations of breathing at the belly for as long as you need.

After some time, notice if the breath feels any different. Where do you feel the breath now? Does the breath still seem fast and shallow? Check into your experience in this moment, has anything changed from the beginning of the practice?

This practice can be as long or short as you need, just continue the practice until you feel the physiological effects settle in, or until you feel better able to proceed with whatever you're doing.
