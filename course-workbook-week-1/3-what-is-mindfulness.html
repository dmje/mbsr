What is Mindfulness?

Autopilot

Perhaps you know the experience of 'going into autopilot' whilst driving, being so lost in thought and distraction that you become completely unaware of the road and what you are doing, and for god only knows how long.

Then you also know that sense of 'coming to', that moment of reconnecting, in a conscious and deliberate way with where you are and what you are doing, noticing the sights and sounds on the road once again, as well as your own thoughts, perhaps even the sensation of gripping the wheel, or your foot on the accelerator pedal (as well as the panic that you've no idea where you are and how on earth you haven't crashed the car!). It's a bit like the difference between being in a dream and being awake.

Much of our waking life can in fact be spent in this autopilot-like state, merely reacting to events, being pushed and pulled around by pleasant or difficult emotions, lost in thinking, thinking, thinking, without being aware that we are thinking: rehashing the emotional events of our past or planning and worrying about the future. It is so easy to drift along in this way, completely unaware of what our minds are doing most of the time, moving unconsciously from one reaction to the next, from thought to emotion to thought, over and over. We may even lose the sense that there is another 'there' to be found, the 'there' of the present moment, where the stuff of life is actually unfolding. 


In a now influential research study, participants were given a buzzer to carry around with them in their daily lives that goes off at random times throughout the day. They were told to, each time the buzzer goes off, report if their mind was on their task or if their mind was elsewhere. The study found that 46% of the time people's minds were wandering.

Absent stress, anxiety or any major source of difficulty in one's life then this autopilot mode of being might be enough to keep you ticking along. However, once stress creeps into our lives, then being on autopilot can feel like being tossed around from one distressing train of thought to the next, with no way off the ride.

So rare can it be that we find ourselves 'coming to', truly connecting with our lives in the present moment, dropping out of our heads and becoming connected to our experience. Yet on these rare occasions, whether its from being out in nature, playing sport, appreciating great art, listening to your favourite song, or just connecting with a loved one, we can feel overcome by a deep sense of ease, of greater connectivity, and fulfilment that offers a tantalising alternative to our ordinary stressed out mental state. However, we are given no guidance in how to snap out of autopilot and reconnect with the experience of our lives in this deeper and more nourishing way.

That is where the tools of mindfulness come in.

Mindfulness

Mindfulness means paying attention in a particular way: …on purpose …in the present moment …non-judgementally Jon-Kabat Zinn 1996

Notice that by this definition it makes no difference what the object of our awareness is at any moment. Some people associate mindfulness with focussing intently on the breath, or with having a completely clear mind, absent thought. In fact, as long as your attention is ‘on purpose, in the present moment and non-judgemental’, we can be doing any manner of things 'mindfully': seeing, hearing, eating, moving, thinking, communicating, laughing, just about anything. The option to be mindful is always there since mindfulness is a way of 'being with' our experience.

MBSR

Jon-Kabat Zinn founded the MBSR course in the 1970's to assist people with stress, anxiety, depression and pain.

The course was set up in a hospital where JKZ was working as a doctor. He asked his colleagues to send him all of the patients who weren't responding to treatment and this was how the success of the MBSR was born.

The course involves a combination of mindfulness meditation practices that we will teach, from body and breath awareness, to movement and walking practices.

The basic principle is simple: to support participants in becoming more present, becoming more awake to their lives, more aware of their minds and bodies, without judgement and reactivity.

When we are experiencing stress in our lives and our minds are left unchecked, the places they end up in are usually also stressful, either worrying about the future or ruminating on past wrongs. This only serves to reinforce the stress in our lives and exacerbate our suffering, both mental and physical.

Through mindfulness meditation we learn to train our minds, returning our minds to the present without judgement over and over again, stops us from getting lost down every dark emotional cul-de-sac of the mind. We also gain freedom, in learning how to respond to our experience in new ways. We learn new ways of being with difficult emotions, building the resilience and the wisdom to navigate our suffering without compounding it.

As we will see, being fully present greatly improves the quality of our lives in innumerable ways. As well as learning the art of meditation, over the course of the 8 weeks we will take a deep dive into the physiology and neuroscience of stress, explore and examine how we each experience stress in our lives, and explore how the practice of mindfulness can cut through the stress response and help us move away from our habitual patterns of relating to stress and experience generally that cause harm.

A practice of mindfulness gives you a deeper connection to the present moment, to the stuff of reality. This is, after all, the only moment you have – past moments are memories, future moments are fantasy – right here and right now is all that anyone will ever have.
