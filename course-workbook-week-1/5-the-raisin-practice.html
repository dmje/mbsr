The Raisin Practice

Before we get anywhere near posture, breathing or anything that looks like meditation, we're going to start with a raisin. Just one.

This is the classic opening exercise of every MBSR course, and there's a reason it has survived unchanged for decades: it shows you, in about ten minutes and with almost no effort, what mindfulness actually is. Not as an idea, but as a direct experience. You can read the definition on the previous page and nod along; but when you eat a single raisin as if you have never seen one before, you feel the difference between thinking about experience and actually having one.

Why start with food?

Because eating is where autopilot lives. Most of us eat the way we do everything else: while reading, scrolling, working, talking, worrying. Food goes in, barely registering. A whole meal can disappear without a single moment of genuinely tasting it.

So we take something about as unremarkable as it's possible to get, and we give it our full attention, as if we'd just arrived on this planet and had never encountered such a thing. The point isn't the raisin. The point is what happens to you while you pay attention to it.

The practice

Take a raisin (a sultana, a piece of chocolate, a nut: whatever you have works) and hold it in the palm of your hand. Then follow these steps as slowly as you can bear.

  1. Look. Really look. Notice the colour, the wrinkles, the sheen, the way light hits it, any lighter patches, the shape. As if you have no idea what it is.

  2. Touch. Turn it between your fingers. Notice the texture, the stickiness, the give, the weight of it.

  3. Smell. Bring it to your nose and take it in. Notice what your mouth does while you smell it.

  4. Listen. Hold it near your ear and roll it between your fingers. You might be surprised how much sound one small dried grape can make.

  5. Taste, slowly. Place it on your tongue. Don't bite yet. Move it around your mouth and notice what's happening: the saliva, the anticipation, the urge to just get on with chewing. Notice that your body has already started eating before you have.

  6. Bite. Just once. Notice the burst of flavour, how it changes, sweet and sharp, where you taste it.

  7. Chew. Slowly. Notice the changing texture, the sweetness building or fading, the point at which swallowing starts to happen almost by itself.

  8. Swallow. Feel it go down. Then pause, and notice: is there still some taste? Some wanting? Something in you already reaching for the next one?

Then, if you like, take a second raisin and eat it the same way, and ask yourself honestly whether the second one was really the same experience as the first.

What people usually notice

Almost everyone is struck by how much there is in one raisin. There is a whole world in there, and it was available all along, but we were busy. That's the first lesson: richness of experience is rarely about adding anything, it's about noticing what's already here.

You may also notice wanting: the mouth watering before you ate, the pull to hurry up. Anticipation is a full-body event. Body and mind are not separate things, and in this small exercise you can watch them working together in real time. That's worth knowing, because this course will come back to that link again and again, especially when we start looking at stress.

And if you notice boredom, or irritation, or a voice saying "this is silly, it's just a raisin": good. Noticing that voice is the practice. That's the tone of mind we're going to be working with for eight weeks, gently.

Beginner's mind

There's a phrase from the Zen tradition that fits here. Shunryu Suzuki wrote: "In the beginner's mind there are many possibilities, but in the expert's mind there are few." A raisin is not something you would ever claim expertise in, and yet notice how quickly the mind files it under "known, boring, irrelevant." Paying attention freshly, as if for the first time, is a skill, and it can be trained.

Taking it further

From today, try eating the first bite of every meal like this. Not the whole meal (that can come later); just the first bite, fully attended. Or pick your morning tea or coffee and give it the same treatment. You'll find the instructions in 6. Home practice for Week One, where we set out your practice for this week, including your daily mindful activity: one ordinary routine, done with the kind of attention you just gave a raisin.
