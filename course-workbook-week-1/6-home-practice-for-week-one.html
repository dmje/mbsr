Body scan

[ todo - reiterate briefly what the body scan is]

In the body scan practice we move our awareness through each part of the body, paying attention to the immediate physical sensations that arise as we go: sensations like tingling, pressure, tightness, softness, tension, vibration, warmth, coolness etc. We simply notice whatever sensations arise in the body with an open, curious and non-judgemental awareness, not trying to change or alter our experience in any way, but accepting experience as it is.

The body scan is a great practice for exploring the ways in which we react to experience. Bodily sensations are either pleasant, unpleasant, or neutral. And during the practice we may notice how we react to pleasant sensations with a sense of grasping or clinging; to unpleasant sensations with tension or fear, trying to push them away; and to neutral sensations by zoning out and getting bored.

By bringing attention to all of this, we can slowly begin to practice experiencing our bodies in a new way, without reactivity, simply allowing the body to be as it is from moment to moment. As a result, we feel more centred and grounded, and can begin to feel at home in our own body.

Bringing attention to the body also allows for new insights; into how our bodies are constantly changing, as solid as they may seem on the surface. When, as a matter of experience we see how each and every sensation is constantly in a process of arising, changing and passing away, we are able to let go of the tendency to cling to the pleasant, and push away the unpleasant.

The assumption that an experience will last forever, once removed, alleviates a tremendous amount of suffering; suffering that we cause ourselves through a simple misunderstanding about how things actually are.

Practice Logs

Keep a log of your activity this week if you think it'll help you. We believe that "self inquiry", i.e. reflecting briefly on your experiences of practice - is an invaluable tool to deepen your experience and learnings from the practice.

Formal practice is time spent taking part in a specific meditation practice – whether a body scan, movement or breathwork meditation.

Informal practice is any time in the rest of you day that you bring mindfulness to an activity, whether eating a meal, taking a shower, doing the washing up etc. We'll go into these in more detail shortly.

Daily Mindful Activity

Try to find an activity in your daily routine that you can practice bringing mindfulness to. It might be eating a meal, taking a shower, going for a walk, doing the washing up, cooking a meal, doing the cleaning etc.

Whatever you choose, try to be as present as you can throughout the activity, staying open and aware, noticing your experience with curiosity as it changes from moment to moment.

Like you did in the raisin practice, try to notice whatever arises just as it is, without judgement, bringing awareness to any immediate sensations and perceptions as well as any reactions in the mind and body, e.g. any thoughts, emotions, urges, judgements etc.

And whenever your mind wanders, just keep gently bringing it back to your immediate experience is as you perform your activity.

Inspiration

In the Zen Buddhist tradition, each morning the monks would roll out a blank piece of parchment, take up their ink brush and opening their mind to whatever their experience in that moment, they would trace out a circle in a single spontaneous brushstroke: a practice called 'Enso' (as shown below). Each figure is completely unique, spontaneous and beautifully imperfect, exactly like each moment of our experience, when we see it clearly.

Similarly, it was popular amongst Zen practitioners to compose short poems of three lines, known as 'Haiku'. Letting go of any ideas, plans or goals for what they were going to write, just keeping the mind open and aware, they would sketch a spontaneous impression of their present moment experience: no frills, just playful and clear.

"The old pond, A frog jumps in, Water sound.""

Matsuo Basho

"First their fronts, Then their backs, Falling maple leaves."

Kobayashi Issa

"Coolness - The sound of the bell As it leaves the bell."

Yosa Buson

Consider your daily mindful activities in this light. Each of these acts is a practice of getting out of our own way, and just allowing each moment to reveal itself in its clarity. Quietly receptive and open to our experience, we put down our expectations, ideas or preferences for how we would like things to be, and receive experience just as it is, with a 'beginners mind', as if experiencing it for the first time.

This is just food for inspiration, it actually helps to keep our daily mindful activities nice and simple at first, preferably those that are already part of your daily routine. But, as you may already have experienced with the raisin practice: just a little mindfulness can transform even mundane acts like eating a raisin, taking a shower, going for a walk, or doing the dishes into something curious, beautiful and even profound.
