The Neuroscience of Mindfulness

Over the past 20 years neuroscientific inquiry has begun to examine the effects and benefits of mindfulness meditation, particularly the MBSR programme. Whilst we cannot completely reduce the effects of mindfulness practice to mere changes in the brain, neuroscience helps us understand the benefits of mindfulness practice, and provides some objective measurement of these changes.

In this section we will briefly outline some of the relevant neurophysiological processes that contribute to our stressed out brains as well as how mindfulness practice works to alleviate that stress and suffering.

The Meditating Brain by Tom Wilkinson

Mindfulness meditation provides us with a technique for zooming out of the midst of our experience, to get a clearer, more objective picture of things. I often think of myself during meditation as a compassionate scientist in the mind, impartially observing my own experience to investigate and uncover its hidden workings. In precisely this way, understanding neuroscience allows us to zoom out even more, to uncover the hidden neurobiological processes and operations that give rise to the thoughts, feelings and sensations that populate our experience.

When we are experiencing stress in the moment for example, understanding the neurobiological process behind it allows us to see the bigger picture and unhook ourselves from whatever personal content is driving the stress, allowing us to focus on more practical ways to manage it. Not only does this give us a deeper understanding of our minds, but it also helps to defuse the tendency to ‘take things personally’ when we act out of stress and reactivity, reducing our habit of blaming and judging ourselves in the process.

Neuroplasticity

‘Neuroplasticity’ is our brains ability to reorganise both its structure and function in response to novel stimuli. For example, when we learn a new ability like playing the piano, or respond in a new way to our environment, e.g. through mindfulness practice, our brain physiologically restructures itself around this new response. You may have heard the phrase 'neurons that fire together, wire together'. For our purposes, what this means is that through repeated habituation of a pattern of thought or behaviour, the neurons responsible for this thought or behaviour will wire themselves together, literally “hardwiring” the changes into our brain. The neuroplasticity of our brains ensures we have the capacity for constant growth and evolution, adapting ourselves to changes in our environment.

To use an analogy, just as the more you exercise your muscles, the stronger they become, well, neuroplasticity means that the more you exercise certain mental responses the more effortless and automatic these responses become. Neuroplasticity reminds us that we really are what we practice, and so if we change what we practice we reshape ourselves in the process.

Take stress as an example, since many of us have spent our lives reacting to stress in harmful ways that we have picked up as early as childhood, the neurons responsible for this reactivity have become wired together, making these reactions our automatic and default way of responding to stress. However, the beauty of neuroplasticity is that, neurologically speaking, things are never set in stone. Mindfulness practice allows us to pause in the face of reactivity, giving us a moment of freedom to choose how we wish to respond. By repeatedly becoming mindful in the face of stress, anxiety or low mood, and finding new ways to respond to these experiences, our brain literally reshapes itself, rewiring itself to make these healthier responses the new default.

This is born out quite dramatically in the scientific research. Studies of long-term meditators have actually shown a physical shrinking in the size of the amygdala, a brain region responsible for emotional reactivity and the initiation of the stress-response. Putting in the time and effort on the meditation cushion is vital to bring about long term changes - studies show that a minimum of 20 minutes of sustained formal practice is required to foster long term neurophsyiological changes (Goleman and Davidson, 2017). However, the power of mindfulness is that it can be applied to every moment of our lives. Each moment is in fact an opportunity to flex our mindfulness muscle and develop more mindful responses to experience.

As the course develops we will learn how to take full advantage of the brain’s neuroplasticity to build healthier and happier minds and brains.

Negativity Bias

Whilst reading about the brain's neuroplasticity may have left you feeling hopeful, I'm afraid to say that reading about the brain's negativity bias might be a bit of a buzzkill. But as we will see later in the course, mindfulness practice provides us with a range of practices that help correct this neurological bias.

Rick Hanson, an author and meditation teacher came up with the following aphorism:

“The brain is like Velcro for negative experiences and Teflon for the positive”

What he is referring to here is another property of the brain known as 'negativity bias'. Put simply, this is a bias our brain has towards remembering and prioritising negative experiences over positive experiences.

Since the brain evolved primarily as a tool to help keep us alive, it tends to err on the side of caution, making sure we remember any negative experiences that might threaten our survival by amplifying their significance in our memory so that we continue to avoid them. In more primitive times, this kind of bias was important - remembering that this particular red berry made you vomit uncontrollably last time you ate it will stop you from making the same mistake over and over again.

POP OUT Research suggests negative experiences take only 5 seconds to be encoded into memory, whereas positive ones take much longer, between 20-30 seconds! So, a positive experience needs to last for 20-30 seconds for you to remember it, whereas a negative experience only 5 seconds! This is remarkable.

Negativity bias ensures we will have a great deal more negative memories of our life than positive. For example, even if today you have actually had more positive experiences than negative experiences, when you look back at your day in retrospect, it is likely to appear as though you had more negative experiences than positive. This is not your fault, it is just the product of our brain's inbuilt negativity bias.

Through this bias the mind weaves a rich implicit tapestry of negative memories along with the associated thoughts and emotions stirred up by them. When something happens that is relevantly similar to one of these memories, then the associated memory as well as the old thoughts and emotions will get reactivated to ensure that you remember the danger and react to avoid harm.

Negativity bias sadly distorts the way we view our lives and consequently the way we view ourselves and our future. Disregarding many of the positive experiences we have had and exaggerating the negative not only puts a gloomy spin on our past, but can easily lead us to the conclusion that our future will be similarly glum. We may become burdened by fear and anxiety with regards to the future, closing ourselves off from new experiences and opportunities that might improve our well-being and help us grow. This further reinforces a depressive outlook and exacerbates depressive moods. But again, this is largely to do with a distortion in our memory rather than the reality of our lived experience.

As we will see in more detail in Week 6 (Insert HYPERLINK), there are a range of mindfulness practices that retrain the mind to pause and fully experience positive events so that we actually remember positive them, counteracting our negativity bias. The amygdala and hypothalamus are believed to play a crucial role in this negativity bias, and as we have already seen, mindfulness meditation reduces amygdala activity and even causes it to shrink in size. Mindfulness meditation also activates the left prefrontal cortex, a region of the brain responsible for a sunnier outlook on life and associated with more positive moods.

The Prefrontal Cortex

  • This brain region is newer in evolutionary terms, being shared only amongst mammals. It is located right at the front of the frontal lobe, just behind your forehead.
  • It is associated with a range of high-level mental processes including cognition, (thought) and executive functions like planning, speech and language, decision making, short-term memory, self and impulse control, as well as complex cognitive processes like rule-following and rational thought.
  • The prefrontal cortex is especially well-developed in humans and primates and so is credited with the distinctive cognitive talents that they exhibit.

We can think of the prefrontal cortex as the part of the brain that helps us to act deliberately, to determine what is in our best interests, make and execute plans in relation to this, and stop us from rashly acting out our emotions.

Amygdala

  • The Amygdala, also known as the ‘Lizard brain’ is one of the oldest regions of the brain, located above the brain-stem, near the base of the brain.
  • It is shared not just with all mammals but also with reptiles (hence the name), evolving long before the prefrontal cortex.
  • It is a vital part of our limbic system responsible for processing emotions and memories, and also for detecting and responding to threats. It plays a key role in our stress-response, working in tandem with the hypothalamus and the Autonomic Nervous System (ANS) to coordinate our mind and body in response to danger.

The amygdala is tasked with constantly assessing our environment for threats and dangers, and in the event of detecting a threat, the amygdala activates the survival circuitry of our Autonomic Nervous System (ANS), our fight or flight response.

As we have seen, when the stress response is activated our amygdala triggers the SNS which floods the brain and body with adrenaline and cortisol that makes us extremely stressed and anxious. As part of this process the amygdala releases hormones that actively inhibit our prefrontal cortex and its higher cognitive functions so that we can act without thinking, coordinating our mind and body to run, hide, or fight. This is essential for our survival. However, it also means that our higher cognitive functions like thought, planning and 'self-control' are not best suited for calming us down in a moment of stress. Even though it's tempting, we can't do it all from up in the head. We need other resources.

This is where our embodied nature picks up the slack. When the head is being inhibited by our stress-response, connecting with our experience of the body, e.g. the breath at the abdomen, or our feet on the floor, activates our parasympathetic nervous system that soothes and calms us so that we can begin to think clearly once again.
