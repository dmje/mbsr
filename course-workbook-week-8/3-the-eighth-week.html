The eighth week

There is a saying in the mindfulness world that the eighth week of the course is the rest of your life.

It is a bit neat, but it is also true. Eight weeks is not long enough to change a life; it is long enough to build a practice that can. What happens now is the real test of everything you have learned: not how you felt in the sessions, but what your practice looks like in an ordinary week when nothing is holding you to it.

What you have actually trained

Looking back over eight weeks, the things you can now do, at least some of the time, include:

  • Steady the attention on the breath and the body, and return it when it wanders, without drama.
  • Work with unpleasant experience, from irritation to genuine difficulty, by turning towards rather than bracing.
  • Notice thoughts as events in awareness rather than as facts you must obey.
  • Use the breathing spaces to interrupt reactivity in the middle of real life, not just on the cushion.
  • Bring awareness into relationship: listening, speaking, and the space between.
  • Nourish the good deliberately, and incline the heart towards kindness, including towards yourself.

That is a formidable toolkit. Like every toolkit, it seizes up with disuse.

Your practice plan for the weeks ahead

Rather than a vague intention to "keep it up", make a plan, and keep it small enough to actually survive contact with your life.

  1. Choose a realistic formal practice. Not the ideal one, the sustainable one. Ten or fifteen minutes daily will hold your practice; forty-five most days will grow it. Decide which you are actually going to do.
  2. Anchor it. Same time, same place, as far as possible. Practice attached to an existing habit, like after the morning coffee, survives better than practice that needs a free half hour to appear.
  3. Keep one informal practice deliberately. Awareness while washing up, one breathing space at a difficult moment of the day. One chosen and done beats ten intended.
  4. Decide what you want from guided practice. Continue with recordings if they support you, or gradually let your own voice lead the practice. Both are fine, and it is not a progression from one to the other.
  5. Plan a review. Put a note in your diary for one month from now: how is the practice doing? Practices rarely die suddenly; they fade quietly while you are busy. A review interrupts the fading.

When practice lapses

It will. Life will take the practice away from you for a week, or a month. This is the moment that decides whether you have a practice for life: a lapsed practice is only a practice that has not yet restarted. Sitting down once after three weeks away is worth more, as practice, than a year of never missing a day.

You do not have to protect your practice like a fragile thing. It will wait, quietly, for as long as it takes. But it will not come and find you. That part, as it has been all course, is yours.
