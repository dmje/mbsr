# Pleasant and Unpleasant Events

## Understanding Craving and Aversion

Perhaps the most basic operating system of the mind is to seek pleasure and flee from pain. In evolutionary terms, it is useful to have something to motivate us towards activities conducive to our survival and reproduction, i.e. pleasure and desire, as well as having a deterrent to push us away from activities that harm us, i.e. pain and aversion. It seems natural for us to view the pleasant as being in some sense ‘good’, and the unpleasant as ‘bad’. Either consciously or unconsciously, we strive to increase our share of pleasant experiences, in the hope of eliminating negative experiences altogether. 

As we will uncover for ourselves in our own mindfulness practice, this view is so deeply ingrained that it conditions almost all of our reactivity and mental habits, acting as the source of much of our suffering. We react to pleasant experiences with **craving/clinging**, grasping at them in the hope that they will last, and we react to unpleasant experiences with **aversion**, resisting and pushing them away.

Viewing pleasant experiences as a sign of success, and unpleasant ones as a sign of regress and failure greatly destabilises the mind. If we experience an unpleasant event when we crave only pleasant ones, then the mind reacts with strong aversion, and, judging unpleasant experiences to be bad, we therefore judge ourselves for having them, feeling like we are not okay, or that we are falling short in some way. We begin to live in fear of negative experiences and they become a trigger for intense stress, keeping our cortisol levels topped up. So we try to avoid them at all costs; developing all sorts of avoidance behaviours and defence mechanisms that we act out so that we don't have to confront the unpleasant - behaviours that harm us and blind us from reality. 

Here’s one you might relate to:

You’re in the office, it’s a beautiful day outside, the sun is out and the sky a clear, vivid blue. You’re feeling buoyed because you just had a nice chat with a colleague in the corridor. But then, as you sit back down at your desk that niggling feeling of anxiety about an approaching commitment that you don't feel prepared for rears its head. The mind reacts with aversion trying to push the thought out of your mind and so you begin procrastinating. Perhaps checking your phone in the hope of extending that breezy feeling of a few moments earlier.

Mindfulness practice gives us a chance to uproot this deeply ingrained habit of reacting to experience with craving and aversion. As is apparent from only a little reflection, unpleasant experiences are simply an unavoidable part of life. Perhaps we fall ill, or a loved one passes away, or we experience some misfortune at work, not to mention the fact that each one of us will ultimately pass away. Life is unpredictable but you can be fairly certain that it will have its ups and downs. If we are incapable of allowing anything unpleasant into our lives, then the stress and the fear that we will face in trying to resist the inevitable swings of life will ultimately overwhelm us.

Placing too much significance on pleasant and unpleasant experiences is the result of not seeing clearly that ***the essence of all experience is impermanence***. Everything changes. We see this clearly in our meditation practice, as we observe the constant ebb and flow of our experience, from pleasant to unpleasant to neutral and back again. And we see it in the process of life itself; we experience hardship and suffering, boredom and restlessness, as well as joy and happiness. 

Realising the impermanent nature of all experience, both pleasant and unpleasant, means we don't have quite so much to fear, things will come but they will also go, and so we find an inner balance and equanimity through life’s ups and downs, resting peacefully in whatever each moment brings. 

## Working with the unpleasant

The process of obsessive craving/wanting after pleasant experiences and strong aversion and fear towards unpleasant experiences plays a crucial role in perpetuating the cycle of stress in our daily lives. Bringing mindful awareness to both pleasant and unpleasant experiences has a range of benefits:

- When we bring mindful awareness to both pleasant and unpleasant experiences, we notice how we react in the mind and body. We can see more clearly the habit patterns of our mind;  where we are holding on, the stories we tell ourselves, as well as our own avoidance behaviours and defence mechanisms. Rather than being choicelessly swept along by our reactions, when we see them for what they are, we can learn to let them go, making space for newer, wiser responses.
- Learning to be with the unpleasant rather than pushing it away, and seeing its impermanent nature, gives us the resilience to stay in the present moment, rather than fleeing or trying to run away when difficulty or suffering arises. We learn to trust in our own basic well-being, a well-being that isn’t pushed around by the changing winds of pleasure and pain, gain and loss. 
- By letting go of the habitual tendencies of desire and aversion, we actually taste true freedom: not wanting things to be different from how they are. This allows the mind to rest, and to be at ease.
