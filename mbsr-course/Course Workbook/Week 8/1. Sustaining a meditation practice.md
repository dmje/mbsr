# Sustaining a meditation practice


> "The 8th week of the MBSR course starts now, but doesn't have an end" 
-- Palouse Mindfulness

For the last two months, we've been collectively involved in a journey together. After this last week together, we part. For some this will be a relief - for others it'll feel strange, maybe even a little bit scary.

So the first thing to say is that we are here for you if you need us. This course is designed so that we can offer you more support if you need it. Don't ever hesitate to reach out.

What we're here to talk about today though is how you might go about sustaining your practice beyond the "formal" part of the MBSR course. You of course don't have to do this - but we hope you do. Many years of formal scientific research but also personal anecdote shows that a sustained meditative / mindful practice can continue to benefit individuals hugely in so many ways. You may have come here to do this course because of a particular source of stress and pain, but even in absence of this stress and pain, a solid mindfulness practice acts as a ballast to help you deal with the inevitable ups and downs of life, bringing greater ease into your life and mind. 

You may also have noticed during the last 8 weeks how the formal practice - that daily 30 minutes of sitting or moving - has started to become a bit more embedded into the rest of your life. We've said a number of times along the way that we're none of us here to become **good meditators**; we're here to get **better at living**. One of the strange things that happens as you make meditation a habit is that the *meditative habit* extends into everyday life. You suddenly find yourself more open to the world, noticing things you hadn't noticed before, you might find yourself talking to a friend and *really listening*, or you drop a plate but you don't get as angry with yourself this time around. 

I find one of the best ways to watch your own growth is in how long your emotions have a hold of you. Ok so you fly off the handle when you come home to find the dogs got out and has pooed all over the house, but instead of brooding over it the whole evening, it only takes an hour for the storm to pass. A bit like the ballast of a ship, the more we practice, the deeper the ballast becomes and the steadier the ship sails, always returning to equipoise even in rough waters.

I like to think of this quality of equanimity as the true core of practice. Certainly, the more you practice, the more you go on growing, discovering more about yourself, tasting more and more freedom, dwelling more in the gap between 'stimulus and response'. The journey is a constant deepening, always unfolding. You might feel overwhelming joy or unbridled peace or moments of pure bliss. But don't get hung up on the highs and lows of practice. Everything changes, and what keeps us grounded in our practice is our understanding of this at every level, over a lifetime, and in every moment. Connecting over and over again with this truth, until it seeps into the marrow of our bones. The truth of change teaches us that wherever we are, however we are, we are already whole right here and now in this moment, the only place there ever will be. 


## Building a habit

When you're first getting started though, it can be easy to find yourself letting things slip - a busy day without meditation can become a patchy week, then a week without meditation, and then before you know it, it's "that thing I tried once"...!

There are a number of approaches which are worth thinking about if you're keen to sustain your habit. And it is - almost - a habit by now! It's reckoned that habit-forming takes around about 65 days, so after 8 weeks of daily practice, you're almost there :-)

But - just to help you over the line, here are a few ‘tips and tricks’ about how to sustain your practice.

1. Sit daily. We'd argue that if you really can’t manage to keep up a longer practice, then a shorter practice that is more regular is better than a longer but more sporadic one. In our experience, it is easier to build momentum in your practice if you practice every single day. Strictly speaking, the science tells us that it takes a daily practice of roughly 20 minutes or more to support the lasting brain changes that meditation affords. If you can manage this, wonderful, but if it's only 10 minutes each day that is workable for you at the moment, then that’s ok. When the benefits of your practice begin to ripen, and you experience it's value more and more, you will slowly commit more time to your practice.   

1. Sit with regularity and build it into your routine. For Mike mornings work well. He is an early riser and can organise his life so that he gets up before everyone else, to practice before the day begins. But however you manage things, try and weave your practice into what you do already to make it stick. If you always exercise at a particular time, then tack on a meditation before that. If you have roughly the same evening routine then tack it into that. Just be careful not to practice too late at night. Meditation is certainly a really useful tool for sleep, but it’s important to distinguish sleep meditation from your regular practice, otherwise you’ll soon find yourself nodding off every time you meditate!

4. Lean into your informal practice. Start by punctuating your day with as many moments of mindfulness as possible. Walking is good - we're always doing it and rarely focus on the walking itself. Just like the breath feel into the flow of sensations in your body as you walk. Practice STOP, feel into your feet on the floor whenever you can. Then over time try to change your perspective so that you see being mindful and connecting to the moment as the default state of your mind, and just remember to keep coming back home.

5. Be kind if you miss a sit. It's ok. Just notice how you feel when you miss a meditation for whatever reason. Feel out any disappointment, or guilt, or perhaps relief. Notice how you feel at the thought of sitting, is there struggle in the mind, aversion or resistance? Are you putting it off or trying to avoid it? Of course this might just be ordinary weakness of will, but it could also be a sign that you are beginning to judge your practice, to view it in terms of success and failure. This is misguided and can be deeply harmful. There is no right or wrong way for our experience to be, everything is always changing. Sometimes our minds are busy, sometimes they are quiet, sometimes there are storms blowing through the mind, sometimes things are more balanced and easeful. Our job is simply to be aware, without clinging, without resistance, to rest as the witness of it all.  


## Reminders

It's easy to get so tied up in your day that you just forget anything at all about mindfulness or meditation. You're just too much in those meetings - and then you realise at the end of the day how mindless you've been! 

Try some of these just to get a little nudge:

1. Set an hourly alarm. Most watches have an hourly bleep or vibrate setting - just use this to trigger a mini "I need to notice" moment. 

2. Use your phone. You can either set a regular alarm, or download one of the many reminder / timer apps. I like OMM- "One Moment Meditation" - it's simple, free, and has a clever name ("Om" is used in yogic and meditative chanting...). This simple app can be set to ping you randomly throughout the day asking you to do a 1 minute meditation.

3. Use a dedicated app. There are scores of these, from the free Insight Timer to Waking Up (my personal favourite) to 10% Happier. Apps can provide wonderfully varied meditations as well as reminders. Many of them are paid, but if you can afford it, well worth doing.

4. Sticker it up! Put PostIt's around your house, with random reminder on them. I've got a "Is this self?" sticker on one of my computer screens and "STOP" on the other - even though they've been there for months I still find myself focusing on them during the working day and pausing for a minute.

1. Build it into your work. Some people like things like Pomodoro timers (these encourage you to work for 25 minutes and then break for 5) - if you're one of these people then use your 5 minutes to do a mini meditation. If you aren't one of these people, why not take breaks between meetings or other tasks and use these for a brief moment of reflection?

## Reinforce

Finally, you can try some things to help reinforce your practice. These include (but are not limited to!):

1. Find a sitting group. There are many of these, both face to face and online. Just do some Googling and see if you can find one which floats your boat. We did some of our training at Sharpham House and they offer a free weekly Zoom session; but many other retreat centres and groups will offer the same sort of thing.

2. Meet. You'll find doing meditation in a group sitting helps you in lots of ways - there’s a sense of solidarity in practicing with others; it can also give your practice more momentum; it’s also easier to sustain if you egg each other on, and you may end up meeting some groovy people, too :-)

3. Read. It's not the same as actually sitting of course, but there is a wealth of excellent meditation books, articles, blogs and more out there. We've attached a suggested reading list below.
