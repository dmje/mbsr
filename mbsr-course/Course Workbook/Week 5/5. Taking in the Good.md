# Taking in the Good

Famously, Rick Hanson, an author and meditation teacher came up with the following:

> “The brain is like Velcro for negative experiences and Teflon for the positive”

What he meant by this is that humans are great at catastrophising, seeing the negative and thinking thoughts like “damn, this always happens to me”. We have something called *negativity bias*. There is science behind this neurobiological quirk - negative experiences take only about 5 seconds to be encoded into memory, whereas positive ones take much longer, between 20-30 seconds! 

The logic behind it is simple. Since the brain has evolved fundamentally as a tool to help keep us alive, it has a tendency to magnify any negative experiences that might threaten our survival, amplifying their significance in our memory so that we continue to avoid them. In more primitive times, this kind of bias was important - hearing a sudden rustling noise coming from behind you probably did mean something nasty was coming to eat you.  

However, this negativity bias can greatly distort the way we view the quality of our lives, leading us to overemphasise the negative aspects of our day and ultimately our lives. 

Think about which bit of your day you’re most likely to focus on - the first 7 hours when your day was nicely ticking along, you got lots done, had lunch with your partner and so on - or the last hour when you got cut up by some guy in his sports car on the way home. We’re Velcro for that last experience - it’s the bit we’d talk about in the bar after work, not the brilliant sunshine or amazing food we ate.

So what we learn here is that humans are not naturally good at noticing the endless stream of positive experiences that we’re surrounded with every day. We have to work much harder to almost force our brains to take on board positive things.

Hanson came up with the phrase Taking in the Good which is as it sounds - a mindfulness technique which can help us to build positive mental and emotional strengths. The technique is simple: we actively spend time focusing on our current pleasant experiences, savouring them in the moment, or recalling pleasant experiences from our day. By spending just 30 seconds or more focusing on these pleasant events, we boost our receptivity to these events, but also “wire together” neurons in our brain, making it easier over time to take in more good. It wears away our Teflon!

Hanson suggests the following specific techniques:

**1) Turn good facts into good experiences.** 

When you notice something good, let yourself feel good about it. It could be as trivial as the smell of your morning coffee, or the smile on the face of a loved on. Allow the feeling to really present itself to you; soak in it for 30 seconds or more. Do this as much as you can each day - try for half a dozen or more. 

Hanson also suggests noticing to see if there is any resistance in yourself to these good feelings. Do you feel guilt, or vanity or shame? Whatever comes up for you, just notice it, without judgement or telling a story about it, and where possible allow yourself to feel good anyway.

**2) Really enjoy it**

Deliberately push the time you’re spending in the enjoyable experience. We’re so used to these experiences just sliding off us - instead, take it the other way and deliberately wallow in enjoyment. Sit with it. Extend the time you spend with it. Go too far!

**3) Intend and sense**

Let the experience become a part of you. Bring it to life by really sensing into the details, what were you feeling in your body at the time, what were you seeing, smelling, thinking, was there a memory that surfaced, really hone into the things that make the experience clear and distinct and just let them be. 

Keep a clear intention to really stay present with the good experience, aware of the good it is doing for you. You might imagine the neurons in your brain forming new pathways, building your ability to stay with the good the more you do it. However you prefer, just know that this practice builds your capability to do this practice better and more automatically - it’s the best kind of virtuous circle.

**4) Gratitude**

Use the last few minutes of your day to focus your attention on the things that went well. Bring these to mind, and again expand your attention so that you spend a period of time on each, really bedding into the experience of taking in the good.

Much scientific research has been carried out on how gratitude affects the way we are. Here are just a few examples:

- Gratitude releases toxic emotions: the hippocampus and amygdala (sites in the brain which regulate emotion, memory and body function) are activated with feelings of gratitude
- Gratitude improves sleep quality - a 2009 study (Zahn et al., 2009) showed that hypothalamic regulation triggered by gratitude helps us get deeper and healthier sleep naturally everyday.
- McCraty and colleagues (cited in McCraty & Childre, 2004), in one of their studies on gratitude and appreciation, found that participants who felt grateful showed a marked reduction in the level of cortisol, the stress hormone. 

## What about pain?

Many of you may be on this course because you suffer from chronic pain, and it’s hard to think about how to see beyond this. But once you enter into this technique, turning towards your pain, and being with it rather than blocking it, you’ll find that you develop a more straightforward relationship with your pain. And with this comes the discovery that there is always something pleasant, even beautiful in your experience when you look for it. 

> “Seeking the pleasant is like being an explorer searching for hidden treasure. It might be as simple as noting the warmth of your hands or a pleasant feeling in the belly, or seeing a shaft of sunlight streaming through the window.”

> -- Living Well with Pain and Illness, Vidyamala Burch / Palouse Mindfulness



## Be Aware, Don’t Cling

Taking in the good is about making up for a neurobiological distortion in the picture we have of our own lives, one that is overly preoccupied with the negative, passing over the positive that already exists. 

We are not striving to get rid of negative experiences and replace them with only pleasant experiences. Nor are we trying to cling desperately to the positive experiences in our day in the hope that they might last longer. Think of this more as an opportunity to explore and become sensitive to the good in your life: become curious about the good that will surface in your life if only you pay greater attention. What sorts of experiences actually bring me joy? And what does that joy feel like? The sounds of children laughing, the warmth of the sun on your face, the way the moon appears through the clouds, the moment you flop down onto bed after a long day? 

The path of mindfulness is about bringing greater awareness to how things already are, tasting the sweetness of experience just as it is. This practice of taking in the good is just this; it shifts us away from the negative framing that we add on to experiences and events, bringing us closer in touch with how things actually are.
