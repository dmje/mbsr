# Learning how to respond?

As seductive as our reactive patterns appear in the moment, our thoughts and emotions are not facts. Even though it can feel so at times, our thoughts do not provide us with a hotline to the truth. The thought that your boss doesn’t like you, or that you don’t deserve happiness are just this, thoughts. Thoughts, feelings and emotions are mental events passing through the mind conditioned by our past experiences and behaviours. The power they have over us is not the power of truth, only the power of habit. By strengthening our mindfulness we develop ways to disengage from this habit of mistaking our thoughts for truths, creating space for new choices and new ways of relating to ourselves and the world. 

Mindfulness teaches us, admittedly somewhat counterintuitively, that it is through the act of turning towards our reactive thoughts and emotions, through simple awareness, that we can be liberated from their influence. 

[[Layers of Experience]] 

Most of the time when we are reactive we are identified with, or functioning from the layer of the story we tell ourselves about experience. We feel sadness, we know we are sad, and then our narrative about sadness comes online: "oh no, this is bad/I'm so weak/etc." We so easily get stuck fixated on our reactive story about experience as if this is the totality of our reality and the truth we must immediately address. But as you can see from this diagram, the vantage of awareness is greater and more spacious than both the immediate experience and our stories about it. 

Your awareness of sadness is not itself sad, your awareness of your thoughts is not itself thinking.

It is only when we drop back into the space of awareness that we can see thoughts for what they are, fleeting stories passing through the mind, conditioned by our past experience, like clouds in a vast open sky. Our thoughts are only a small part of our experience, a bit like a radio playing in the corner of the room that we can choose to tune in and out of. But we need to practice tuning in and out, flexing our mindfulness muscle. Mindfulness practice helps us to inhabit the whole room, to drop back into the more open space of awareness in which you are no longer identified with the story about experience. Seeing our thoughts and reactions from the layer of awareness teaches us to hold our stories with lightness, instead of automatically believing the content, and in this space of awareness we allow room for new responses to arise.


> “Between stimulus and response there is a space. In that space is our power to choose our response. In our response lies our growth and our freedom.” 
 Victor Frankl

Recognising these 3 layers involved in our reactions is important for helping us navigate our lives with greater flexibility and intentionality. Here are some tricks for helping us stay aware:


**Note it** - 
- The reactive mind fights dirty, dredging up highly emotional, implicit memories and appealing to insecurities, negative narratives and beliefs about ourself that we have had since childhood to grip our attention and keep us highly activated. In other words, it's very persuasive!
- One useful tool to help us maintain awareness amidst difficult experiences is to internally make a soft mental note of any experience that appears in awareness. We note these internally to ourselves without judgement or agenda, just observing, as if taking a roll call of experience: fear... struggle... thinking... self-judgement... anger... tightness… tingling... warmth... confusion...
- This act of ‘mental noting’ hones and centres our awareness whilst maintaining space between us and the content of the painful thought or emotion.
- Noting is a bit like taking an experience right in front of your mind’s eye, placing it in a picture frame and pinning it up on the wall in front of you - the experience goes from immediate and all-consuming, to an item of curiosity that we can relate to differently.
- This is a great tool to use in your STOP practice when you are noticing your experience.

**Embodiment** 
- Remember - we humans are embodied creatures. Although it might not feel like that when we are stressed out and stuck in our own heads, the body is always right here, anchoring us to the present moment. 
- Strong emotional reactions are always felt in the body as patterns of physical sensations. Whether full-blown emotions like anger, fear, or anxiety, or more subtle feelings like aversion, struggle, or resistance, each of these experiences appears in the body as a pattern of physical sensations (a 'somatic profile'). For example, anxiety might manifest as ‘tightness in the chest, heaviness in the limbs, jaw clenching, flushing in the face, tension in shoulders, tingling sensations in the hands and feet’. 
- Learning to drop out of the storyline about an emotional reaction and just become aware of the changing pattern of physical sensations showing up in the body is a great way to stay grounded to the present moment in the midst of reactivity. After all, sensations are simply sensations, it is the story we give them that exacerbates our reactive mind. This is one of the best practices for becoming more resilient in the face of difficult experiences. (Remember bodily awareness engages our PNS!)
