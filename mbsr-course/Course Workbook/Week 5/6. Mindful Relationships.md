# Mindful Relationships

Our relationships play a tremendously important role in our lives. Mindfulness meditation introduces us to quiet and stillness as a way of being, however, as some of you may have noticed already, we are often at our most reactive around other people, whose behaviour is completely beyond our control. Engaging in difficult communications with other people is an unavoidable part of life, and particularly when we are stressed, over-tired or just running on autopilot, other people act as the catalysts for our reactive minds to take over. Whether it be our parents, our children, our partners, close friends, or even our pets, I’m sure many of us can bring to mind countless episodes where we lost it or lashed out at these individuals.

When we are struggling to cope with the stressors in our lives, we can sometimes close ourselves off from others and become so preoccupied with just coping that we pass over the reality of others’ feelings and needs. Not being able to listen adequately to the emotions and needs of others greatly damages the quality of our relationships, increasing our feelings of alienation and isolation which are a strong predictor of poor health, both mental and physical. 

Having strong relationships is one of the single greatest predictors of wellness, happiness, and longevity.

## Fostering Mindful Relationships

Bringing mindful attention to both ourselves and to other people during the process of communication can dramatically improve our connectedness to others, and the quality of our relationships. 

As we discover more about ourselves through mindfulness practice, we learn how to break out of autopilot and let go of the habitual patterns of thought and behaviour that trigger our stress-response. This brings with it a new sense of freedom and new choices in how we interact with others. Even if it’s as simple as not raising your voice so much when you feel angry, or taking a breath before replying, mindfulness opens up new possibilities for us. 

We also develop a more resilient and stable ground from which to remain present with difficult experiences. This is an absolute gamechanger in relationships. It allows us to remain open to other people’s suffering, really listening to and accepting their difficulties without closing ourselves off through fear of experiencing something unpleasant and getting overwhelmed or activated. This can be as simple as not trying to fix whatever problems a friend might want to talk to you about, or avoiding the urge to put a sunny spin on their difficulties, or overly focusing on the positives.

> “To listen entails a fundamental letting go of self-centeredness. We have to be willing to put down our own thoughts, views, and feelings temporarily to truly listen.”

> -- Oren Jay Sofer

One simple practice: Bring to mind a situation in which you experience some reactivity in a close relationship. Perhaps it is typical or representative of this relationship in some way. Maybe you end up getting angry or hurt, or you end up upsetting the other person. Now ask yourself these simple questions to uncover the root of this reactivity and discover a place from which to respond differently:

**What was the intention/motivation behind your speech or action?** 

- E.g. Was there an unmet need? Perhaps you felt misunderstood, ignored? Perhaps you were trying to protect yourself? Did you want to take control of the situation? Did you want to be right? Be listened to, seen, or validated?
- Now offer kindness to this part of yourself, understanding the place from which you reacted with acceptance and compassion.

**Ask the deeper question “what *really* matters here?”**

- What is your deepest intention with this person?
- If you were at the end of your life, what would really matter to you in this interaction?
- How might this deeper intention help guide you? 
- How might you respond differently from this intention? 
- What other choices might you have?

Being stressed out and stuck in autopilot not only closes us off from others but also from our deeper and more authentic intentions. By pausing more in our daily lives to discover and reconnect with our most authentic intentions, we can remember what really matters to us, bringing our actions and thoughts into greater harmony with our deepest truth. 

The more we practice this, the more we weaken our old neural habits that bring harm to ourselves and others, and the stronger the neural pathways that tap into our deepest values and most authentic intentions become.
