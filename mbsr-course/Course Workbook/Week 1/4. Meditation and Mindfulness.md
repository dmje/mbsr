# Meditation and Mindfulness


>Someone asked a Zen Master, "How do you practice Zen?"
The master said, "When you are hungry, eat; when you are tired, sleep."
"But isn't that what everyone does anyway?"
The master replied, "No, No. Most people entertain a thousand desires when they eat and scheme over a thousand plans when they sleep."

As we progress through the 8 weeks we will deepen our understanding of mindfulness meditation, mostly through our own practice and our experiences on the cushion. 

However, it is worth trying to dispel some of the most common misconceptions about mindfulness meditation.


#### Not thinking 

Most people think of meditation as 'emptying the mind', achieving a state of total inner quiet, absent thought or distraction of any kind. Although such states of deep concentration are possible during practice, this is by no means the goal of mindfulness practice. Mindfulness meditation is simply being open and aware of what is here moment after moment. We are not trying to change our experience in any way, or bring about certain experiences over others. Our minds think, that's what they do, they will get distracted, bored, anxious, annoyed, irritated, completely and utterly lost in thought for minutes at a time on the cushion. We are not trying to 'not-think', say by pushing thoughts away or trying to block them out of experience. During meditation, when the mind is thinking then we acknowledge that thinking is here, and we simply *notice* this thinking, letting go of the storyline or narrative we have been caught up in, before returning to the present moment through some object of awareness, like the breath or the body. 

In the words of John Brehm,

> "When we sit in meditation, we shift from *thinking about* our life to *experiencing* whatever arises in the present moment: bodily sensations, sounds, feelings, the breath. Thoughts will happen, too, but we learn not to indulge them or chase after them. We simply notice them as one more strand in the intricate texture of the present moment."


#### Blissing Out

Another common misconception is to think that mindfulness meditation is about blissing out on special inner experiences, deep states of peace, joy, rapture and tranquility. This is certainly a tempting view of things. After all, we are all here looking for some benefits of the practice, of inner growth, healing, or freedom from suffering. So surely there is some 'there' that we are aiming at.

It is certainly true that these states of bliss, inner peace and tranquility are available through practice. However, they are not what our practice is *about*. If we aim our practice at experiencing certain states over others, then we aren't really being mindful at all and so we won't receive the benefits of the practice. Mindfulness is not about trying to get anywhere else. In fact we let go of our desire for things to be different and we open to what is actually here, without judgement. However things are in this moment we simply open to them *as they are*. Sometimes our experience is delightful, sometimes it is rather painful, experiences come and go and when we recognise this we find an inner balance and equanimity. 



#### Self-Indulgent Escapism

Another common misconception of meditation practice is to see it as a way of escaping, retreating from the outside world and all of your problems by turning inwards into your own inner world. Mindfulness meditation is far from an escape from your problems, it is actually quite the opposite. Our minds are the source of much of our suffering, so when we sit and pay attention to it, it is the same ordinary mind that shows up, with all its usual worries, fears, insecurities, and anxieties that we know so well. The healing power of meditation is in *turning towards* our experience in each moment, however we find it. Whether it is pleasant or unpleasant, turning towards our experience with the attitudes of non-judgement, equanimity, acceptance and patience, helps us to be less reactive, giving ourselves the space and freedom to *respond more skilfully* to our problems, or to what Jon Kabat-Zinn calls 'the full catastrophe' of our lives.
