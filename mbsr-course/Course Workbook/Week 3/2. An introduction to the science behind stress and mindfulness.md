# An introduction to the science behind stress and mindfulness


Alright, let’s take a bit of time to look into the science behind stress, and how mindfulness practice works to alleviate stress. It’s not going to get too heavy, don’t worry – but it’s useful to understand a bit about the physiological and scientific foundation of both stress and mindfulness.

We all have a nervous system. This is a hugely complex part of animals that transmits signals around the body. The nervous system is broken down into different parts, which perform different functions, and the part most relevant to stress is called the Autonomic Nervous System or ANS.

The ANS is responsible for a whole range of physiological processes, most of which are involuntary or unconscious. Things like breathing, digestion, heartbeat – for example: you don’t normally think terribly hard about these, but your body just gets on and does what it needs to do. Some other processes that the ANS oversees are:

### Example ANS processes

- Digestion
- Blood pressure
- Heart rate
- Urination and defecation
- Pupillary response
- Breathing (respiratory) rate
- Sexual response
- Body temperature
- Metabolism
- Electrolyte balance
- Production of body fluids including sweat and saliva
- Emotional responses


Some of these processes the ANS controls need to be used by an organism to help its chances of survival in certain situations. For example, increasing our heart rate pumps oxygen to our muscles around the body that can help us to act more favourably for our survival. The ANS is therefore broken down into three subsystems which can be activated separately under certain conditions: **the enteric nervous system** which we won’t be considering any further here; **the sympathetic nervous system** or SNS, and the **parasympathetic nervous system** or PNS.

Broadly speaking, the SNS is considered a “quickly responding system” that mobilises the body for action as in 'fight or flight' situations, whereas the PNS acts more slowly in order to dampen our arousal response and calm us down once the threat has passed.

So coming back again to our “savanna” environment: you’re walking along and hear a rustle in the bushes and then a tiger jumps out leaping towards you. Your SNS system kicks in, mobilising your body into action through adrenaline, muscle stimulation, quickened heartbeat, and so on. Once you’ve run and (hopefully!) escaped your threat, your PNS would then come into the picture, acting to dampen down these responses and return you to a normal, resting, relaxed state.

The more common (and slightly easier) names for these two systems are:

SNS: “fight or flight”
PNS: “rest and digest”

At the heart of the neurobiology of these systems is the amygdala – known to be the oldest part of the brain - the 'lizard brain'. We'll return in more detail to the amygdala later on, but basically the amygdala is responsible for deciding when to activate our autonomic nervous system (ANS), i.e. when a situation is threatening enough to activate our fight or flight response. Because the amygdala is such an old part of the brain however, it functions independently of the brain's newer cognitive functions like conscious reasoning, planning etc. This means that the amygdala can activate the ANS and hijack our brains and bodies completely *involuntarily*, i.e. whether or not we want it to. This is why in fight or flight situations our body just moves, and by the time we are fully aware of what is happening we are already running from danger. 

We do of course need the SNS – our threats nowadays probably aren’t tigers jumping out, but seeing a car careening towards you in the street, smelling something on fire or spotting a confrontation heading your way – these still require rapid, subconscious responses and movement. In order to survive we need to be able to jump out of the way and to react quickly without having to think about it first.

But: our amygdala's can become oversensitive and modern life can appear jam-packed with things that ***continue to trigger the SNS*** even though they aren’t tiger-, fire- or oncoming traffic-sized threats to our survival. If you’re late for a meeting, got a presentation you haven’t quite prepared for, forgot to pack your kid's PE kit – these can all have your SNS kicking in, flooding your body with all the “fight or flight” responses you would have needed to escape that tiger once upon a time.

For some of us our lived experience can feel like a game of pinball bouncing from one trigger of the SNS to the next. Whether it's from stress at work or home, from relationships, physical illness or injury or declining mental health, one stress can lead to another until we end up locked in a cycle that keeps us physically and mentally impaired. So how can we learn to manage an overactive amygdala and SNS?

## What does this all have to do with mindfulness??

Ok. Without further ado, here’s the kicker: **mindfulness practice activates the PNS**.

Here it is again – because it may seem a little weird: **mindfulness practice activates the “rest and digest” part of our nervous system**. 

The overwhelm that many of us can feel in modern life can be the result of bouncing from one stress-inducing situation to the next, keeping our sympathetic nervous system activated. This creates a nervous system that is out of balance: our SNS finds itself in a state of constant arousal, but we haven't the tools to engage our PNS to calm ourselves down properly.

Amongst the many benefits of mindfulness practice is this curious physiological fact –  that paying simple, mindful attention to our moment to moment experience of the body and the breath activates a basic part of our nervous system designed to slow us down, chill us out and return us to a rested, relaxed state, in which we can feel safe.
