# The Two Arrows


Steve wakes up early Monday morning. He’s got a busy week ahead. Lots to do at work, and all the normal stuff at home to sort out, too. Kids, lunches, rubbish, cars, dogs.

He opens his eyes and sits up. The moment he does, he realises he’s got a headache. A blinder. One of those ones that is going to be there all day, no matter what he does.

He slumps back into bed, overcome by misery, knowing that his day is going to be overshadowed by this pain behind his eyes.

Here are some of the thoughts that come to him…

- *“Damn it, why me?”
- *”I didn’t even have a drink all weekend, and see what happened!”*
- *“Not again!”*
- *“It’s not fair, this always happens”*
- *“I can’t believe this is happening when I’m this busy”*
- *“Is there something wrong with me?”*
- *“This is the third time I’ve had a headache this month!”*
- *“How long will this last?!”*
- *“It really, really hurts”*
- *“I can’t think straight!”*
- *“How am I going to get through the day?!”**

Let’s examine what’s going on here.

Firstly, there’s a headache. This is unavoidable, primary suffering. It’s just life – and as much as life is full of joy and happiness, it’s also got plenty enough pain and suffering as well. We’re going to get headaches, we’re going to lose jobs, miss opportunities, fall out of love, end up fighting with the people we like the most, and more.

> “As long as we live and love, there will be suffering”

Life comes at us from every direction – some of it is beautiful and lovely, some of it is painful and difficult. This rich pageant is what makes our journey.

So… Steve can’t avoid his headache. But he’s just piled on a big old handful of reactions to his unavoidable headache – struggle, catastrophising, resistance, guilt, anxiety, tension, frustration and more. 

This was illustrated to me in a rather lovely way at the first retreat I went to. The same example was given – someone was asked to stand in the middle of the room and told to imagine they had a headache. The tutor gave them a cushion to hold, representing the headache. Then we went around the room of about 20 people, and each of us called out a secondary response to the headache, and each time the tutor gave another cushion to the person. By the time we’d got around the room the person was sweating under the weight of all the cushions – and the point was then made that only the very first one was the actual, unavoidable cause of suffering. All the others were created, imagined, exaggerated by our reactions to the first.

Now, the point here is not to say that this secondary suffering that we add on top of the initial suffering isn’t real. It is, and for many of us it is as real as the primary suffering caused (in this instance) by the headache. But: we have a level of control over the secondary causes of suffering we inflict upon ourselves, and by training the mind through mindfulness, we can learn to deal more skillfully with the unavoidable, primary cause of suffering so that we reduce the number of secondary, avoidable causes of suffering.

The traditional way of talking about these two sources of suffering is called *The Two Arrows*. This comes from a Buddhist parable about dealing with suffering more skilfully. The parable says that when we suffer a painful experience, two arrows fly our way: the first arrow strike is the unavoidable pain - painful things hurt; but the second arrow strike is all of the avoidable, secondary mental and emotional suffering we add on top of the original pain - things like catastrophising thoughts that lead to anxiety ‘how am I going to get through the day?’, or taking it personally by harshly judging yourself ‘is there something wrong with me?’. Strictly speaking, there is no end to the number of second arrows that can follow the first, it just depends on how far down the rabbit hole you have fallen before you catch yourself!

A related Buddhist parable talks about two monks – an older and younger one – who approach a river where there is a woman standing by the bank. She is trying to get across. Even though they have taken vows that forbid them to touch anyone of the opposite sex, the older monk offers to carry the woman across the river and she agrees. He does so, sets her down and the monks go on their way. The younger monk is obviously distressed about this break in protocol but for an hour he says nothing as they walk to their destination.

After this time he can’t keep his feelings in any more and blurts out: “We are not permitted to touch women! How could you possibly do what you did?!”

The older monk looks at him and replied “Brother – I set her down at the side of the river. Why do you still carry her after all this time?”.

The lesson here is wider than that provided by *The Two Arrows* but broadly it carries the same resonance: we will have to take on board many things during our lives, but we can learn lessons about accepting difficult experiences and letting go of reactivity which are profound and can help hugely with alleviating our suffering.

## Where Mindfulness Comes In
 
This second column is where we have a choice. By using the range of techniques we’re learning on this course, we can put space between these columns, noticing and accepting the first arrows, and giving ourselves more agency to choose how we are in response to these.
 
>“Pain is inevitable. Suffering is optional.”
~ Unknown, possibly the Dalai Lama…
 
Vidyamala Burch in her 2008 book From Living well with Pain and Illness identified two branches of secondary suffering. She suggested that we often “block” or “drown”.
 
**Blocking behaviour** includes…
 
- Restlessness
- Addiction (of all kinds) – drink, sex, drugs, excess
- Being emotionally brittle and edgy
- Denial
- Becoming controlling
 
**Drowning behaviour** includes…
 
- Exhaustion
- Physical inactivity
- Giving up
- Depression
- Self-pity
- Tendency to catastrophise
 
All of these are *resistance* to the primary arrow. As we get better at acceptance, at *turning towards*, we become more aware of our second arrows and learn to let them go, becoming more equanimous in the face of difficulty.

Avoiding the second arrow becomes a three step technique: 

First, *notice* the first arrow. Be aware of your pain, accept it and allow yourself to let it in, really feel it - whether emotional or physical. Be fully aware of what that pain is like, know that you are suffering, and bring some kindness to yourself and the situation. 

Second, notice and focus on your emotional and cognitive reactions - any second arrows. Do you feel angry and want to shout or yell, or anxious and want to run or crawl up into a ball, or cry? Have you started judging yourself, or started catastrophising about just how bad the situation is? Become aware of any additional pain and suffering, any resistance to the first arrow, and try to be present with it all, allowing it to be there, again bringing kindness to yourself.

Finally, give yourself credit for having gone through this process. Congratulate yourself for this act of kindness and for achieving some mastery over yourself.
