# Qualities / Virtues of Interpersonal Mindfulness

Cultivating strong relationships is a challenge. When our relationships become strained or go through a rocky patch it is easy to lose perspective, fall back on old self-protective defense mechanisms, or turn away completely from them. The research suggests that good social relationships (just one or more) are the biggest predictor of happiness and well-being, as well as being associated with better health and longevity. 

Jon Kabat-Zinn, the author and creator of the MBSR tells students:

>“Make sure you weave your parachute every day, rather than leave it to the time you have to jump out of the plane."  

This goes for all of our mindfulness practices, both our formal practices sitting on the cushion as well as our informal practices that we weave into our daily lives. Our relationships are no different. Dropping out of a more self-centred perspective and into a compassionate and open-hearted mindset requires cultivating certain qualities *as* *often as we can*. 

Goldstein and Stahl (reference) highlight 6 qualities they consider essential in cultivating interpersonal mindfulness and deepening our relationships:

### Openness 
Similar to beginner’s mind, this is a quality where you aren't closed off to the reality and perspective of another person. Instead you are open to seeing the other person as well as the relationship with new and fresh eyes.  

### Empathy 
The first step to empathy is to be able to recognise your own feelings and then you can do this with other people. Intuiting others' feelings can be effective, and combats our own inner narrative which tends to be pretty 'self-preoccupied', shutting us off from other people. 

### Compassion 
This quality combines empathy and a wish to ease the suffering of another. Imagine the person as your own child and how you would comfort them. 

### Loving Kindness 
This is a quality where you truly wish others well- to be healthy, safe, free from harm and free from fear. 

### Sympathetic joy 
This is a quality in which you delight in the happiness and joy of others. 

### Equanimity 
Equanimity is a quality of wisdom, an evenness and steadiness of mind that understands the nature of change and the interconnectedness of things. Equanimity gives you the balance and composure to let things be as they are, to not hold on so tightly. 

It is helpful to think of equanimity as in a sense foundational to the ripening of these other qualities. For example, feeling sympathetic joy and taking delight in another person’s happiness requires a mind with the steadiness to let go of your own immediate concerns. 



Although cultivating these states of mind dramatically boosts our own well-being, it is useful to practice cultivating these qualities as small acts of generosity that can really make a difference not just to your own life, but to the lives of others. We experience them without wanting to receive anything in return. If we seek to cultivate these interpersonal qualities only in order to make ourselves happier, or only to improve the quality of our lives, we will miss the point. To show up for others requires 'getting out of your own way'; being open and receptive to other people *for their sake*, not just for our own. 


I would also add the following: Vulnerability, honesty, respect, unconditional positive regard.


## Happiness is not a ‘Zero-sum Game’

When we are buckling under the weight of our stresses it is easy to fall into a **scarcity mindset**. With a scarcity mindset we see life as a finite pie. So when someone takes a chunk of the pie then there is less for everyone else. 

Here is an example we might be able to relate to:

Jim, who is single, meets his friend who announces that he has recently entered into a loving relationship. But instead of being happy for his friend, somewhere deep down Jim feels a sense of loss and mild anxiety at the feeling of being left behind. 

A scarcity mindset views another’s gain as being at the cost of our own. It is also sometimes known as ‘zero-sum thinking’. This kind of thinking can start from as early as childhood, where children adopt this mindset with respect to their parents' love for them; perceiving a display of love for another sibling as taking away from the love available to them. 

Sadly, we can also fall into a scarcity mindset with respect to happiness. In this case, we perceive someone else’s happiness as taking from the finite pie of available happiness, leaving less for everyone else, but most importantly for ME! 

This kind of thinking is deeply misguided and a barrier to building strong relationships. It can bring up a lot of difficult human emotions, like pride, jealousy, competitiveness, bitterness that build, yep, stress. 

The truth is that happiness is not a zero-sum game - someone else's happiness does not come at the expense of your own. Happiness is in fact a limitlessly abundant resource; a state that is available to anyone at any time. The more we remind ourselves of this, the less threatened we are by other people’s joy and happiness, and the more able we are to connect with the virtues of interpersonal mindfulness, finding joy in another’s joy or acting to alleviate another’s suffering without seeking anything in return.
