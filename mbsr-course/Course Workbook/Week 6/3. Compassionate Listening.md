# Compassionate Listening

How many times in your life have you been in a conversation with someone and truly listened? I mean just *listened*. Not been preoccupied with what you are going to say next, or trying to formulate your own opinion of the issue, not judging the speaker or what is being spoken, not thinking of how to get your own way, not being distracted, not trying to solve the problem, not thinking of ways to steer the conversation towards something of more personal interest, not listening simply in order to be listened to in return.

(If this is a regular occurrence for you, then truly I applaud you, you should probably be teaching this section of the course instead of me.)

I should imagine that for most of us, this is a fairly radical event. For those with little experience of meditation, the monkey mind can be so busy and unchecked that being able to listen to someone in this way, with undivided attention, and a deep and pure intention just to listen is a rare experience. 

Now can you think of a time *when you were listened* *to* in this way by another person? Unless you have managed to befriend some exceptionally talented listeners, then I imagine being listened to in this way will be an occurrence at least as infrequent as you listening to others in this way. 

To be given the space and time to say whatever it is you want to say, without being made to feel like you have to rush to get it out before you are interrupted, is an extremely compassionate act. It is an act of giving, a generosity. Listening is a surprisingly *active* task, it requires awareness of oneself as well as the other person and it requires a compassionate intention to put your experience to one side and give yourself as fully as you can to understanding the experience of another person. I like to call it ‘compassionate listening’, but you might also call it ‘mindful listening’, or ‘deep listening’.

It is also quite difficult. In order to listen to someone else, we have to listen to ourselves. The mind can be a very noisy place. There is a lot going on in our experience at any one moment, as I'm sure we have realised from our time on the meditation cushion: bodily sensations, thoughts, emotions, feelings, urges, desires, expectations. When we listen, we need to be aware of all this, along with the other person, holding it all lightly without letting it distract us. All of the urges to interrupt, how we feel about what is being said, the distracting thoughts, as well as the stories and interpretations we begin to tell ourselves, we need to let it all go, and bring ourselves back to the other person, reconnecting with our intention just to listen, over and over again.

According to Alidina (2010), in order to listen deeply and mindfully:

- You must stop doing anything else and set your intention to listen deeply. 
- Always look the person in the eye when she/he speaks. 
- Focus entirely on them, putting aside all your own concerns, worries and agenda. 
- Really listen to what the person is saying and how he/she’s saying it. 
- Listen with your whole being, your mind and heart, not just your head. 
- Be aware of how your posture and tone of voice is part of the listening process. 
- Notice your own automatic thoughts as you listen and try to let them go and come back to listening. 
- Keep your questions genuine and open rather than trying to change the subject, gently deepening the conversation. 
- Let go of judgement, judgmental thoughts, as far as you can. 
- Let go of trying to solve the problem, you do not need to give the person the answer.
