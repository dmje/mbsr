# Sustainers and Drainers

As we grow into our mindfulness practice and integrate more mindfulness into our daily lives, we might still feel a lingering sense of ‘being stuck’, excessively drained and burnt out by the end of each day. No matter how deep your commitment and intention to being mindful is, it is so important to think on a practical level about how best to structure your daily life in order to best support your growth and development. 
 
Becoming aware of the effects our daily activities have on our well-being allows us to see which of these activities actually nourish and **sustain** us, and which of those **drain** us, obstructing our ability to be more mindful.  

**Drainers** – Activities that decrease your sense of being present and connected. Perhaps they emotionally drain you, contribute to a feeling of physical or mental exhaustion, keep you on autopilot, or make you feel less able to cope with your suffering.
 
**Sustainers/Nourishers** – Activities that increase your sense of being present and connected. Perhaps they make you feel more supported, more easeful, better able to cope with life's suffering and challenges, or just more awake and alive.

You could do this chronologically, starting from when you wake in the morning to when your head hits the pillow at night. Try to be fairly specific, e.g. if you commute to work, include what you are doing during the commute.
 
Now take a moment to reflect on whether, for each activity on the list, you consider it to play a ‘sustaining’ or ‘draining’ role on your well-being.
 
Importantly, some activities might be sustaining in one way or in some context but also draining in another way or context. For example, you might find socialising with friends or family to be both sustaining and draining in different ways. You can write either ‘S’, ‘D’ or ‘Both’ opposite each activity.

## Reflections
 
There are 3 main things to reflect on here.
 
1. How can we consciously increase the time and effort we give to sustaining activities that nourish and revitalise us?

2. How can we decrease the time and effort spent engaged in draining activities that compound stress, keep us on autopilot, and keep us hooked up to conditioned reactions that increase our suffering?

3. In addition to the unnecessary drainers, there will also be draining activities that are simply unavoidable parts of our day - aspects of our daily life that we cannot change. It is important to recognise this fact. Even so, we can still consider how we might approach these activities with greater mindfulness, how to be more grounded and present, and how this might mitigate the negative effect they have on us.
 
 
Now make a list of as many sustaining and draining activities that you can think of. 

These can range from small things like remembering to connect with the feeling of your feet on the floor, sitting more upright in your chair, staying hydrated, to more behaviourally demanding things like eating something healthy and nutritious for lunch, going for a stroll during a break from work, or reading a book on your commute to work instead of scrolling through Instagram or news feeds.

Having a broader range of sustaining activities to choose from can help you adjust your ‘sustainers’ to the changing business of daily life.
