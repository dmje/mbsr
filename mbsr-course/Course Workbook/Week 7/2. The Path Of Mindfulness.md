# Why Practice Compassion: The Path of Mindfulness

![[the-path-of-mindfulness.excalidraw.svg]]


1. Practising kindness towards oneself and others stimulates serotonin which calms anxiety and acts as a natural antidepressant.

2. Compassion practice produces endorphins which lifts our mood and gives us more vigour and energy.

3. Compassion practice reduces cortisol levels and contributes to what scientists call a ‘broaden and build’ response to stress. Rather than a fight or flee response, the broaden and build response involves seeking cooperation and building relationships in the face of stress. This theory suggests that acting on compassionate impulses has several beneficial effects including increased resilience and reducing negative emotions.

4. Feeling compassion produces ‘oxytocin’, sometimes known as the ‘love hormone’, which makes us feel more connected to others as well as more optimistic. Oxytocin is also known as a ‘cardioprotective hormone’ that reduces blood pressure and promotes heart health. 

Practising compassion can radically transform our perspective on ourselves, other people, and life more generally, allowing compassion to flow naturally from our view of things. 

When we begin to recognise the human predicament in all its fullness, the natural response is to view ourselves and others with compassion. We are all, each of us, in this moment the result of countless influences beyond our control. We are the products of millions of years of evolution and genetic development. We are subject to conditioning from our parents, our family, our early experiences as well as the influence of our wider society and environment of which we are a part. 

All of these factors shape our ‘personalities’, conditioning our thoughts and emotions, and forming the mental habits and patterns of behaviour that ultimately create suffering for us. By the time we are self-conscious agents with a set of values by which to try to more deliberately shape ourselves, we are already in the full swing of what Jon Kabat-Zinn calls ‘the full catastrophe’ of our lives. 

When we begin to appreciate how embedded we are in a complex, interconnected web of causes and conditions stretching back beyond the cradle, we realise that ***the way we are is not our fault***. The way we are, the way we react, the way our decisions have unfolded, none of this arises from our own choosing or from some clearly defined ‘self’. 

Most of us have automatically taken to blaming and judging ourselves for all that we think, feel and do, and for just generally being imperfect creatures, prone to failure. We see it all as a reflection of our ‘self’, and we think of the millions of other ways we would rather be. But when we acknowledge the full reality of our situation, what does this blaming and judging even mean? Do the concepts of blame and judgement even make sense when we see our lives from this deeper perspective?

When we take a good look at the human predicament, and we see others who just like us, wish to be happy in spite of their own suffering born of countless influences beyond their control, we see the need to be kinder, more accepting and more forgiving of both ourselves and others, opening up the space for compassion.
